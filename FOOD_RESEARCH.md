## 1. Banku

### What it is
Banku is a Ghanaian staple "swallow" — a smooth, soft, slightly sour dough cooked from fermented maize (corn) dough, usually blended with fermented cassava dough. It is portioned into individual balls and eaten hot. The fermentation gives banku a clean, mild tang that carries peppery soups, stews and grilled fish well. It is strongly associated with southern and coastal Ghana (Ga, Ewe and Fante communities) but is eaten across the country.

### Ingredients and preparation
Fermented maize dough and fermented cassava dough are mixed with water and salt, then cooked over heat while being stirred continuously with a wooden paddle until the mixture thickens into a smooth, stretchy, cohesive dough. The dough is kneaded, portioned, and shaped into balls. Fermentation of the maize and cassava typically takes several days. Related local names include akple (Ewe) and ɛtsew (Fante).

Sources:
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banku_(dish)
- https://memanghana.com/food/banku-fermentation-strength-and-ghanaian-flavour
- https://ghanaianfoods.com/how-to-make-banku-at-home-a-step-by-step-guide/

### Traditional accompaniments
Banku is never eaten alone. It is served with a soup, stew or pepper sauce, and a protein. Classic pairings:
- Banku with okro (okra) stew, often with fish, crab or meat.
- Banku with grilled tilapia and pepper sauce (shito), one of the most recognised Ghanaian pairings.
- Banku with groundnut soup, palm nut soup, light soup, or plain ground pepper with fried fish.

### Possible menu variations
- Banku with okro soup + choice of protein (fish, crab, goat meat).
- Banku with grilled tilapia (medium/large) + pepper sauce.
- Banku with ground pepper and fried fish.
- Banku with groundnut soup or palm nut soup.

## 2. Fufu

### What it is
Fufu is a starchy, elastic dough made by pounding boiled cassava and unripe plantain together in a mortar until smooth. In southern Ghana it is typically cassava and plantain; in parts of the north, yam is used instead. It is moulded into balls and eaten with the fingers by tearing off small pieces and dipping them into soup. Fufu cannot be eaten alone — the soup is what gives the dish its identity.

### Ingredients and preparation
Cassava and unripe plantain (or yam in some regions) are peeled, cut into pieces and boiled until tender, then pounded in a large wooden mortar with a pestle while being turned and sprinkled with water, until smooth, elastic and stretchy. It is shaped into balls and served immediately with hot soup.

### Traditional accompaniments
Fufu is always served with a soup and a protein:
- Fufu with light soup (tomato, onion, pepper base, often with fish, chicken or goat).
- Fufu with groundnut (peanut) soup (nkatenkwan), rich and creamy.
- Fufu with palm nut soup (abenkwan).
Common proteins: chicken, goat meat, beef, smoked fish, crab, snail.

### Possible menu variations
- Fufu with light soup + chicken or goat meat.
- Fufu with groundnut soup + chicken or goat meat.
- Fufu with palm nut soup + goat meat or beef.
- Cassava fufu vs plantain fufu (where the business offers both).

## 3. Omotuo (Omo tuo)

### What it is
Omotuo is the Ghanaian rice ball — soft rice cooked well past tenderness, then mashed and folded until the grains bind into a smooth, cohesive, glossy dough that is shaped into balls about the size of a fist. It is sometimes called "rice balls" or "rice swallow". In Ghana it is strongly associated with Sunday lunch and is served with soups rather than eaten alone. A related dish exists in northern Nigeria and Niger as tuwo shinkafa, but in Ghana it is paired with groundnut and palm nut soups.

### Ingredients and preparation
Short-grain or broken rice is cooked in plenty of water until very soft and sticky (the starch is not rinsed away, so the grains bind). The hot rice is beaten and folded against the side of the pot with a wooden spoon until smooth and elastic, then shaped into balls with wet hands or a small bowl. It firms as it cools, so it is served immediately.

Sources:

### Traditional accompaniments
Omotuo is served with soup ladled around (not over) the balls:
- Omotuo with groundnut soup (nkatenkwan) — the classic pairing.
- Omotuo with palm nut soup (abenkwan).
- Omotuo with light soup.
Common proteins: chicken, goat meat, beef, smoked fish, snail.

### Possible menu variations
- Omotuo with groundnut soup + chicken or goat meat.
- Omotuo with palm nut soup + chicken or goat meat.

## 4. Kokonte (Konkonte)

### What it is
Kokonte is a swallow made from dried cassava flour (kokonte flour), also known locally as abetiɛ, lapiiwa or "face the wall". Cassava is cut into chips, sun-dried, and milled into flour; the flour is stirred into boiling water and worked into a smooth brownish-grey dough. It is a lean-season staple from northern and Volta Ghana that keeps for months as flour, and is especially associated with the Ga-Dangme, who call it "dziji". It is darker and more earthy than fufu or banku.

### Ingredients and preparation
Kokonte flour is stirred into boiling water and beaten continuously with a sturdy spoon until smooth, elastic and glossy, pulling away from the pot in a single mass. It is portioned into balls and served hot with soup. Commercial kokonte flour is paler; traditionally sun-dried chips give a browner colour.

### Traditional accompaniments
Kokonte is served with a rich soup and protein:
- Kokonte with groundnut soup (the classic partner — the richness balances the plain dough).
- Kokonte with palm nut soup.
- Kokonte with okro stew or light soup.
Common proteins: chicken, goat meat, tripe, fish, crab.

### Possible menu variations
- Kokonte with groundnut soup + chicken or goat meat.
- Kokonte with palm nut soup + chicken or goat meat.

## 5. Rice dishes

### What it is
Rice is a central part of Ghanaian cuisine, most famously as jollof — a one-pot dish in which rice cooks directly in a seasoned tomato-based stew until every grain absorbs the flavour, giving it a reddish-orange colour. Ghana also has fried rice (rice stir-fried with vegetables and protein, adapted to Ghanaian seasonings), plain boiled rice served with tomato stew or palm-oil "oiled rice" (angwa moo), and rice balls (omotuo, covered separately).

### Ingredients and preparation
- Jollof rice: rice, tomatoes, tomato paste, onion, bell pepper, scotch bonnet, ginger, garlic, thyme, curry powder, bay leaves and stock, cooked in one pot with a protein (chicken, beef or goat).
- Fried rice: parboiled rice stir-fried with vegetables (peas, carrots, green beans, onion) and a protein, seasoned with Ghanaian spices.
- Rice with stew: plain boiled rice served with a separate tomato stew and protein.
